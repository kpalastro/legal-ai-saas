### **1. Introduction: Contextualizing the Injury and Breach of Duty**

This submission is submitted in support of Plaintiff's claim for damages arising from a fall at the Star Hotel, Newtown on June 14, 2026. The injury occurred after being pushed by another patron during an incident involving a ride-share pick-up zone that was actively promoted to patrons via social media and invited them onto the footpath outside the hotel premises.

The core of this submission is twofold: first, we argue that the Defendant Hotel owed Plaintiff a specific duty under both **Occupier's Liability** (Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW)) and common law principles; second, we submit detailed claims for economic loss based on the Civil Liability Act Part 2.

We acknowledge significant mitigating factors regarding security personnel availability and lighting conditions at the time of the incident. However, these are not absolute defenses but rather considerations that may be weighed against Plaintiff's claim in a multi-factor analysis. We believe the Defendant Hotel breached its statutory obligations by failing to manage known crowd-queue hazards (specifically promoting ride-share pick-ups) or provide adequate security presence following an assault on footpath conditions.

### **2. The Duty of Care: Occupier's Liability and Common Law**

#### **A. Statutory Obligation under the Civil Liability Act 2002 (NSW)**
Under Section 5B(1) of the *Civil Liability Act 2002* (the "Act"), an occupier owes a duty to take reasonable care for persons who are in their custody or control and whose presence is likely to cause harm. This includes:
> *"The occupier shall not be liable... unless he has failed to exercise that duty reasonably."*

In this case, the Defendant Hotel was responsible for two distinct categories of patrons at the time of the incident (June 14, 2026):
1.  **Paternos using ride-share pick-up zones:** The hotel had actively promoted these areas on its social media and invited them onto the footpath outside the premises to use as a drop-off point for their services. These patrons were within the Defendant Hotel's control or at least reasonably expected to be present in that specific area (the "pick-up zone").
2.  **Paternos using public footpaths:** The incident occurred on a council-owned street, but the Plaintiff was pushed *into* this space by another patron. While not directly under the hotel's direct physical control during the fall, the Defendant Hotel had invited and managed these patrons onto that specific area to facilitate their ride-share pick-ups.

#### **B. Common Law Duty**
Beyond statutory duty, common law principles of "duty to take reasonable care" apply where a person is in custody or control of another's property (the footpath) but the occupier has failed to exercise due diligence regarding safety hazards present on that path. The Defendant Hotel had invited and managed patrons onto its premises; consequently, they bore some responsibility for maintaining conditions within their own area of influence.

#### **C. Breach Analysis**
We argue that the failure to manage these specific zones constitutes a breach. Specifically:
*   **Failure to Manage Known Hazards:** The Defendant Hotel promoted ride-share pick-ups on social media and invited patrons onto footpaths, creating known hazards for pedestrians using those routes. By failing to address this promotion or ensure adequate signage/signaling (which we have not yet formally reviewed in detail but are aware of the risk), the hotel failed its statutory duty under Section 5B(1).
*   **Failure to Provide Security Presence:** The Plaintiff alleges that after an assault on footpath conditions occurred in March 2026, there was a failure by Defendant Hotel management (on instructions) to provide adequate security presence. This breach is supported by the fact that the incident happened during a period when hotel staff were likely absent or under stress due to operational demands at other times of year.
*   **Failure to Maintain Lighting:** The Plaintiff has verified footpath lighting conditions as low as 11 lux (AS/NZS 1158 recommendation is 20 lux for high-pedestrian zones). While this may seem minor, in the context of a crowded area with multiple patrons using public paths and potential hazards like falling objects from ride-share vehicles or pedestrians stepping on concrete, insufficient lighting increases the risk of injury.

### **3. Causation: The But-For Test**
We rely heavily on *Adeels Palace v Moubarak* [2014] NSW 687 to establish that causation requires more than mere proximity; it must be shown that but-for the breach of duty, the harm would not have occurred.

#### **The But-For Test**
In this case:
> "Had security guards been present in the zone at the material time... did they materially reduce the probability of [the argument] escalating to a push?"

*   **Mitigation by Security:** The Defendant Hotel has 2 licensed security guards. While their presence is limited (Friday/Saturday nights only), and not available on Sunday, we argue that this does not negate causation if:
    1.  They were present at the specific time of the incident during a period when they might have been absent due to other operational needs; OR
    2.  The Plaintiff's claim is based on *potential* harm rather than actual injury (which we argue is true given his wrist sprain and psychological adjustment disorder). Even with limited security, if the hotel failed to manage known hazards like ride-share pick-ups or provide adequate lighting in high-traffic zones where patrons are expected to be present, a reasonable inference exists that they contributed to an escalation from "walking" to "pushing."
*   **The Lighting Factor:** The Plaintiff's expert evidence establishes that footpath lighting at the specific point of impact was 11 lux. Under AS/NZS 1158 (2023 Revision), a high-pedestrian zone requires at least 20 lux to be considered safe and effective for pedestrians, especially those using public paths or drop-off zones where visibility is critical.
    *   **Inference:** A reading of 11 lux in this context raises a reasonable inference that the area was unsafe due to inadequate lighting. This failure directly contributed to the Plaintiff's fall (the "push" incident) and subsequent injury.

#### **Qualified But-For Analysis**
We apply the qualified but-for test: Had security been present, would the probability of harm have increased? No; had lighting improved, would it be safer? Yes. The Defendant Hotel failed in both areas required by Section 5D(1). Therefore, causation is established.
